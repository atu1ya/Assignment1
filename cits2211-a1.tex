\question{3}
A rule is sound if every truth assignment making all its premises true also makes its conclusion true.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item \textbf{Unsound.} Take $P=\T$ and $Q=\F$. The premise $P$ is true but the conclusion $Q$ is false.
\item \textbf{Sound.} If $P$ and $Q$ are both true, then $P\vee Q$ is true.
\item \textbf{Unsound.} Take $P=\T$ and $Q=\F$. The premise $P$ is true but $P\leftrightarrow Q$ is false.
\item \textbf{Sound.} If both premises are true, then $P=Q=\T$, so $P\leftrightarrow Q$ is true.
\item \textbf{Sound.} The premises $\neg P$ and $P\vee P$ cannot both be true, since $P\vee P\equiv P$. Hence there is no truth assignment where all premises are true and the conclusion is false.
\end{enumerate}

\question{6}
We prove the contrapositive: for every integer $a$, if $a-1$ is not odd, then $a^3$ is not even.

Let $a\in\mathbb Z$ and suppose $a-1$ is not odd. Every integer is either even or odd, so $a-1$ is even. Therefore, for some $k\in\mathbb Z$,
\[
a-1=2k,\qquad\text{and hence}\qquad a=2k+1.
\]
Cubing and expanding gives
\begin{align*}
a^3&=(2k+1)^3\\
&=8k^3+12k^2+6k+1\\
&=2(4k^3+6k^2+3k)+1.
\end{align*}
Since integers are closed under addition and multiplication, $4k^3+6k^2+3k$ is an integer. Thus $a^3$ has the form $2m+1$ for an integer $m$, so it is odd and therefore not even.

This proves the contrapositive. Hence, for every $a\in\mathbb Z$, if $a^3$ is even, then $a-1$ is odd.

\question{7}
Let $S(n)$ be the statement
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{n}i^3=\frac{n^2(n+1)^2}{4}.
\]
\textbf{Base case:} For $n=1$, the left-hand side is $1^3=1$ and the right-hand side is
$1^2\cdot2^2/4=1$. Therefore $S(1)$ holds.

\textbf{Inductive step:} Let $k\geq1$ be an integer and assume $S(k)$ is true, so
\[
\sum_{i=1}^{k}i^3=\frac{k^2(k+1)^2}{4}.
\]
Then
\begin{align*}
\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}i^3
&=\sum_{i=1}^{k}i^3+(k+1)^3\\
&=\frac{k^2(k+1)^2}{4}+(k+1)^3
&&\text{by the inductive hypothesis}\\
&=\frac{(k+1)^2\bigl(k^2+4(k+1)\bigr)}{4}\\
&=\frac{(k+1)^2(k^2+4k+4)}{4}\\
&=\frac{(k+1)^2(k+2)^2}{4}.
\end{align*}
This is the formula for $S(k+1)$. Therefore, by mathematical induction, the result holds for all natural numbers $n\geq1$.

\question{8}
The impossible positive values are $1,2,3,5$. If $0$ is included in $\mathbb N$, it is also impossible. Thus, independently of the convention for $\mathbb N$,
\[
X=\mathbb N\cap\{0,1,2,3,5\}.
\]
Here each smaller square has positive side length strictly less than that of the large square, and the smaller squares cover it without overlapping interiors.

\textbf{Impossible cases.}
Zero squares cannot cover a square. One square could cover it only by being the same size, so $n=1$ is impossible.

Each corner of the large square must be occupied by a smaller square whose edges at that corner lie along the outer edges. These four corner squares are distinct: one containing two outer corners would have the same side length as the large square. Therefore at least four smaller squares are required, ruling out $n=2$ and $n=3$.

To rule out $n=5$, suppose the large square has side length $s$. Let the four corner squares have side lengths $a,b,c,d$, at the bottom-left, bottom-right, top-left and top-right respectively. Non-overlap along the four outer sides gives
\[
a+b\leq s,\qquad a+c\leq s,\qquad c+d\leq s,\qquad b+d\leq s.
\]
\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
Any strict inequality leaves a positive-length boundary gap that must be covered by the fifth square. To cover such a gap, that square must have an edge on the outer boundary, so its edges are parallel to those of the large square. It cannot cover gaps on opposite sides without having side length $s$, or on adjacent sides without occupying their common corner. Hence it can cover a gap on at most one outer side.

At least three of the four inequalities must therefore be equalities. Rotating or reflecting the diagram if needed, take these to be
\[
a+b=s,\qquad a+c=s,\qquad c+d=s.
\]
The first two give $b=c$, and the last two give $d=a$. The area remaining for the fifth square is then
\[
s^2-(a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2)
=(a+b)^2-2a^2-2b^2
=-(a-b)^2\leq0.
\]
But the fifth square must have positive area. This contradiction rules out $n=5$.

For a unit square, the $n=6$ construction uses one square of side $2/3$ and five of side $1/3$. The $n=7$ construction divides one of the four quarters into four squares. The $n=8$ construction uses one square of side $3/4$ and seven of side $1/4$.

\textbf{Induction.}
Let $S(n)$ mean that a square can be tiled by exactly $n$ smaller squares. Whenever $S(n)$ holds, divide one constituent square into four equal squares. This replaces one square with four, so the new count is $n-1+4=n+3$. Thus
\[
S(n)\ \Longrightarrow\ S(n+3).
\]
We now use strong induction to prove $S(n)$ for every integer $n\geq6$.

\textit{Base cases:} $S(6)$, $S(7)$ and $S(8)$ hold by the constructions above.

By strong induction, every $n\geq6$ is possible. Together with the construction for $n=4$ and the impossible cases proved above, this establishes exactly the stated set $X$.

\question{9}
We prove $P(\phi)\geq2M(\phi)$ by structural induction on the construction of $\phi$.

\textbf{Base cases:} If $\phi$ is $a$, $b$ or $c$, it contains no $|$ or $\times$ symbols. Therefore $P(\phi)=M(\phi)=0$, so the inequality holds.

\Needspace{11\baselineskip}
\textbf{First construction:} Suppose $\phi=\psi|\chi$ and assume
$P(\psi)\geq2M(\psi)$ and $P(\chi)\geq2M(\chi)$. This construction adds one $|$ and no $\times$, so
\[
P(\phi)=P(\psi)+P(\chi)+1,
\qquad M(\phi)=M(\psi)+M(\chi).
\]
Using the inductive hypotheses,
\begin{align*}
P(\phi)&\geq2M(\psi)+2M(\chi)+1\\
&\geq2\bigl(M(\psi)+M(\chi)\bigr)=2M(\phi).
\end{align*}

\Needspace{13\baselineskip}
\textbf{Second construction:} Suppose $\phi=\psi\times\chi||\delta$ and assume the inequality holds for $\psi$, $\chi$ and $\delta$. This construction adds two $|$ symbols and one $\times$, so
\begin{align*}
P(\phi)&=P(\psi)+P(\chi)+P(\delta)+2,\\
M(\phi)&=M(\psi)+M(\chi)+M(\delta)+1.
\end{align*}
Therefore, by the three inductive hypotheses,
\begin{align*}
P(\phi)&\geq2M(\psi)+2M(\chi)+2M(\delta)+2\\
&=2\bigl(M(\psi)+M(\chi)+M(\delta)+1\bigr)\\
&=2M(\phi).
\end{align*}
All base cases and both construction rules preserve the inequality. Hence structural induction gives $P(\phi)\geq2M(\phi)$ for every formula $\phi$.

\question{10}
Write $\Nzero=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ for non-negative integer indices.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \textbf{Natural numbers that are multiples of 7.} Both notations can be used.
\[
M_7=\{n\in\mathbb N\mid \exists k\in\Nzero,\ n=7k\}.
\]
For a recursive definition, start with $0\in M_7$ if $0\in\mathbb N$, or with $7\in M_7$ if the natural numbers start at $1$. If $n\in M_7$, then $n+7\in M_7$. No other elements belong to $M_7$.

\item \textbf{Prime numbers.} Predicate notation gives
\[
\mathrm{Pr}=\left\{n\in\mathbb N\ \middle|\ n>1\ \wedge\
\forall d\in\mathbb N\,\bigl(d\mid n\ \to\ (d=1\vee d=n)\bigr)\right\}.
\]
Here $d\mid n$ means that $d$ divides $n$. There is no comparably simple recursive rule that generates primes from earlier primes without a further primality condition, so I use predicate notation.

\item \textbf{Binary palindromes.} Both notations can be used. In predicate notation,
\[
\mathrm{Pal}=\{w\in\{0,1\}^*\mid w=w^R\},
\]
where $\{0,1\}^*$ is the set of finite binary strings and $w^R$ is the reversal of $w$.
Recursively, $\varepsilon$, $0$ and $1$ belong to $\mathrm{Pal}$, where $\varepsilon$ is the empty string. If $w\in\mathrm{Pal}$, then $0w0$ and $1w1$ belong to $\mathrm{Pal}$. No other strings belong to $\mathrm{Pal}$.

\item \textbf{Factorial numbers.} Define the sequence recursively by
\[
f(0)=1,\qquad f(k+1)=(k+1)f(k)\quad(k\in\Nzero).
\]
Then the set of factorial numbers is $F=\{f(k)\mid k\in\Nzero\}$. Predicate notation is also possible using a finite product:
\[
F=\left\{m\in\mathbb N\ \middle|\ \exists k\in\Nzero,\ m=\prod_{j=1}^{k}j\right\},
\]
where the empty product, for $k=0$, equals $1$.

\item \textbf{Square numbers.} Predicate notation gives
\[
\mathrm{Sq}=\{n\in\mathbb N\mid\exists k\in\Nzero,\ n=k^2\}.
\]
A recursive description is also possible: let
\[
q(0)=0,\qquad q(k+1)=q(k)+2k+1\quad(k\in\Nzero),
\]
and take $\mathrm{Sq}=\{q(k)\mid k\in\Nzero\}\cap\mathbb N$.
The recurrence works because $(k+1)^2-k^2=2k+1$.
\end{enumerate}

\question{12}
Recall that
\[
T=\{(i,j)\in X\times X\mid\exists k\in X,\ (i,k)\in R\wedge(k,j)\in S\}.
\]
\textbf{(a) True.} Assume $R$ and $S$ are reflexive. Let $i\in X$ be arbitrary. Reflexivity gives $(i,i)\in R$ and $(i,i)\in S$. Choosing $k=i$ in the definition of $T$ gives $(i,i)\in T$. This holds for every $i\in X$, so $T$ is reflexive.

\textbf{(b) False.} Let $X=\{1,2,3,4,5\}$ and take
\[
R=\{(1,2),(3,4)\},\qquad S=\{(2,3),(4,5)\}.
\]
Both relations are transitive. In $R$, no pair starts at $2$ or $4$, so there are no two pairs $(x,y)$ and $(y,z)$ in $R$. Similarly, in $S$, no pair starts at $3$ or $5$. Thus neither relation has a pair of composable edges that could violate transitivity.

Composing $R$ followed by $S$ gives exactly
\[
T=\{(1,3),(3,5)\},
\]
using the intermediate elements $2$ and $4$ respectively. Since $(1,3)\in T$ and $(3,5)\in T$ but $(1,5)\notin T$, the relation $T$ is not transitive. This disproves the statement.
